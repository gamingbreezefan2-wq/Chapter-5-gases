5.1

Gases and pressure

Gases expand to fill the shape and volume of their container, have low density, are compressible, and mix completely regardless of identity — because their particles are far apart and move constantly, fast, and in all directions. When moving particles strike a surface they exert a force on it; pressure is force per unit area.

P = forcearea

Torricelli's barometer (17th century): atmospheric pressure pushes mercury ~760 mm up an evacuated tube. A manometer is a U-tube of liquid; the fluid level drops in the higher-pressure arm and rises in the lower-pressure arm.

Pressure units

1 atm = 760 mmHg = 760 torr = 101,325 Pa (N/m²) = 14.696 psi = 1.013 bar
Exam trap. Convert h to the same unit as Patm first (9.2 cm = 92 mmHg). Decide + or − by looking at which arm is higher, not from memory.
5.2

The gas laws of Boyle, Charles, and Avogadro

Boyle's law (constant T, n): PV = k. P vs V is a hyperbola (V doubles → P halves); V vs 1/P is a straight line through the origin with slope k. Precise measurements show PV is not exactly constant — deviations grow at higher pressure. A gas that obeys Boyle's law is called an ideal gas.

Charles's law (constant P, n): V = bT, T in kelvin. Every gas's V vs T line extrapolates to V = 0 at −273.2 °C, defined as 0 K (absolute zero). The dashed extrapolations below cover regions where real gases would already have liquefied.

Avogadro's law (constant T, P): V = an. Equal volumes of gases at the same T and P contain the same number of particles (followed closely at low pressure).

Exam trap. Every one of these proportions uses kelvin. 25 °C → 50 °C does not double anything (298 K → 323 K).
5.3

The ideal gas law

PV = nRT
R = 0.08206 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹ = 8.31451 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹

A gas that obeys this equation behaves “ideally”. Real gases approach ideal behavior at low pressure and high temperature. When n is fixed, write it for two states and cancel nR:

P1V1T1 = P2V2T2  (combined gas law)

Any held-constant variable simply cancels, giving Boyle, Charles, or the P–T relation automatically.

Exam trap. Match R to your units: with 0.08206 use atm, L, K, mol. Pressures given in torr/mmHg must be converted to atm (÷ 760) before using R — but in a ratio like the combined gas law, any consistent pressure unit cancels.
5.4

Gas stoichiometry

STP = 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm. The molar volume of an ideal gas at STP is 22.4 L. Chapter 3 stoichiometry combines with the gas laws: amounts of gas are often given as volumes (with P and T stated).

ConditionShortcutExample for 2A + B → 3C
P and T constantVolume ratios = mole ratiosVA2 = VB1 = VC3
V and T constantPressure ratios = mole ratiosPA2 = PB1 = PC3

Molar mass and density

Substituting n = m/M into PV = nRT and using d = m/V:

P = dRTM   ⇔   M = dRTP

At constant P and T, a larger molar mass means a larger density.

Exam trap. 22.4 L/mol is only valid at STP. At any other T or P use PV = nRT.
5.5

Dalton's law of partial pressures

In a mixture, the total pressure is the sum of the pressures each gas would exert alone (its partial pressure). For ideal gases only the total number of moles matters — not particle identity, size, or the forces among them.

Ptotal = PA + PB + … = ntotalRTV
χ1 = n1ntotal = P1Ptotal  ⇒  P1 = χ1Ptotal

Collecting a gas over water

A gas collected by water displacement is mixed with water vapor whose vapor pressure depends only on temperature:

Ptotal = Pgas + Pwater vapor
Exam trap. Subtract the water vapor pressure before using PV = nRT for the collected gas. And “50% by mass” is not “50% by moles”.
Bio link. Alveolar air follows Dalton's law: PO₂ = χO₂·Ptotal. At altitude χO₂ is still 0.21, but Ptotal falls, so PO₂ falls.
5.6

Molecular speeds (kinetic molecular theory)

Gas particles do not all move at the same speed. The root-mean-square velocity, most probable velocity, and average velocity describe the distribution:

urms = √3RTM    ump = √2RTM = √2kBTm    uavg = √8RTπM
R = 8.314 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹, M in kg/mol, kB = 1.38066 × 10⁻²³ J/K
ump : uavg : urms = 1.000 : 1.128 : 1.225

The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution f(u) arises from the many collisions and kinetic-energy exchanges among particles. As T increases, the curve shifts right (faster on average) and becomes broader and lower (wider spread of speeds).

SpeedMeaningFormula
umpspeed of the greatest number of particles (peak)√(2RT/M)
uavgarithmetic mean speed√(8RT/πM)
urmssquare root of the mean of u²; always the largest√(3RT/M)

Mean free path: the average distance a particle travels between collisions — very small, about 1 × 10⁻⁷ m for O₂ at STP.

Exam trap. Divide g/mol by 1000 to get kg/mol. Forgetting this makes the speed wrong by a factor of √1000 ≈ 31.6.
Bio link. Average kinetic energy depends only on T (KEavg = 3/2 RT per mole) — at body temperature every gas in your lungs has the same average KE, but light molecules move faster.
5.7

Diffusion and effusion

EffusionDiffusion
DefinitionPassage of a gas through a tiny orifice into an evacuated chamberMixing of gases
Rate measuresHow fast gas transfers into the chamberHow fast gases mix
Classic demoGas escaping through a pinhole into vacuumNH₃ and HCl cotton plugs → white NH₄Cl ring forms nearer the HCl end

Graham's law (found experimentally): at the same T and P, relative effusion rates are inversely proportional to the square roots of the molar masses. The rate depends directly on the average particle velocity — faster particles hit the orifice more often.

rate1rate2 = √M2√M1     time1time2 = √M1√M2
Exam trap. Time is the inverse of rate — the heavier gas takes longer. Sanity check: the lighter gas must come out faster.
5.10

Real gases (textbook background)

An ideal gas is a hypothetical concept. Real gases follow PV = nRT closely only at low pressure (and more closely at high temperature). The kinetic-molecular model assumes particles have zero volume and no interactions — both false for real gases. Van der Waals corrected for each:

[Pobs + a(nV)²](V − nb) = nRT
Ideal gasReal gas
Particle volumeZeroFinite → available volume is V − nb
AttractionsNonePresent → particles hit walls less often; Pobs lower by a(n/V)²
PV/nRTExactly 1≈ 1 only at low P; deviates at high P, low T
Best approximated at—Low pressure, high temperature
